How loud is 110 dB?

110 dB is painfully loud for many people. Approximate comparisons include an emergency-vehicle siren up close, the front of a rock concert, a chainsaw at the operator's ear, or a car horn a meter away. NIDCD lists emergency sirens at about 110–129 dBA, and concerts or headphones at maximum volume up to about 110 dBA. A given siren or show can be quieter or louder than that.

What about 110 dB sounds like

Compared with 100 dB, 110 dB has about ten times the sound energy and is usually heard as about twice as loud. Talking is close to impossible.

Using the NIOSH method (85 dBA over 8 hours, time halved for every 3 dBA), 110 dBA is about 1.5 minutes a day. In practice, unprotected time at this level is a hearing risk even when it is brief.

Phone and laptop microphones can clip or compress very loud sound, so a browser meter may read lower than the true level. Treat the number as an estimate and assume the real level may be higher.

Hearing

Move away or wear hearing protection. CDC notes that one very loud sound near the ear, and repeated loud exposure, can cause noise-induced hearing loss.

Published health guidance is context. It is not a verdict on a recording from a phone.

Measuring with SOUNDTEST.PRO

The browser meter gives an estimate from the microphone in the phone or computer, not a reading from a certified sound level meter. The product's accuracy page describes a typical field spread of about ±1 to 5 dB after the built-in device baseline. Use it to compare times and keep a dated note. A watermarked PDF preview is included; removing the watermark is paid. CSV export of the session figures is available from the meter. For an official, workplace, or enforcement measurement, use a professional meter and the method that rule names.
