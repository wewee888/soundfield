dBA vs dBC: Key Differences & When to Use Each

The difference between dBA and dBC is how they treat bass and low-frequency rumble. While dBA filters out low frequencies to match human hearing at quiet speech levels, dBC remains flat across bass frequencies to capture structural thumping, subwoofers, and HVAC resonance. Understanding both is essential for investigating noise disputes.

What is dBA (A-Weighting)?

dBA applies an acoustic weighting curve modeled after the human ear's equal-loudness sensitivity at moderate sound levels (specifically the 40-phon Fletcher-Munson contour standardized in IEC 61672-1).

Because human ears are anatomically less sensitive to deep bass and very high pitches, A-weighting aggressively reduces low-frequency energy in its calculation:

  • At 1,000 Hz: 0 dB adjustment (reference point).
  • At 500 Hz: -3.2 dB reduction.
  • At 125 Hz: -16.1 dB reduction.
  • At 63 Hz: -26.2 dB reduction.
  • At 31.5 Hz: -39.4 dB reduction.

Because most municipal environmental noise ordinances and occupational rules (such as OSHA and WHO guidelines) were written around speech interference, dBA is the default statutory metric worldwide.

What is dBC (C-Weighting)?

dBC applies a substantially flatter acoustic frequency weighting modeled on human auditory response at high sound pressures (around 100 phons). At high volumes, the human ear perceives bass frequencies much closer to their true physical energy.

C-weighting leaves low frequencies almost completely unattenuated between 31.5 Hz and 8,000 Hz:

  • At 1,000 Hz: 0 dB adjustment.
  • At 125 Hz: Only -0.2 dB reduction.
  • At 63 Hz: Only -0.8 dB reduction.
  • At 31.5 Hz: Only -3.0 dB reduction.

As a result, dBC captures deep acoustic energy that dBA hides: bass guitar, car subwoofers, HVAC fan vibrations, diesel engine idling, and mechanical plant thumping.

The Diagnostic Rule: When dBC − dBA ≥ 10 dB

One of the most practical diagnostic tools in acoustic engineering is measuring both weightings at the same moment and calculating the difference:

Low-Frequency Dominance Criterion:

If (dBC − dBA) ≥ 10 to 15 dB, the acoustic environment is heavily dominated by low-frequency energy (bass rumble, structure-borne vibration, or mechanical drone).

This explains a very common dispute: A neighbor or bar plays music with heavy sub-bass. Inside your bedroom, walls and windows filter out mid and high frequencies (treble), leaving only the low-frequency bass vibrating your room. An officer or landlord uses an A-weighted meter, sees 38 dBA (well below the 45 dBA legal curfew), and dismisses the complaint. Meanwhile, a C-weighted measurement reveals 54 dBC—proving massive bass energy is entering the room.

Frequently Asked Questions About dBA vs dBC

What is the main difference between dBA and dBC?

dBA (A-weighting) mimics the human ear's sensitivity at moderate volumes by heavily cutting low-frequency sounds below 500 Hz (down -26 dB at 63 Hz). dBC (C-weighting) maintains an almost completely flat response between 31.5 Hz and 8,000 Hz, capturing deep bass, structural vibration, subwoofers, and industrial machinery rumble that dBA ignores.

When should I measure with dBC instead of dBA?

Use dBA for general neighborhood noise compliance, daytime speech interference, and standard workplace safety (OSHA/NIOSH). Use dBC when documenting bass thumping from clubs or stereos, HVAC chiller humming, generator rumble, elevator motor vibration, or nighttime ceiling impact noise where low-frequency sound penetrates walls.

Why does low-frequency bass noise disturb people even when the dBA reading is low?

Because human walls and windows block high-frequency sounds easily while letting low-frequency acoustic energy pass through with minimal loss. When a deep bass hum enters a bedroom, A-weighting heavily filters out those low frequencies, resulting in an artificially low dBA reading. When the difference (dBC minus dBA) is 10 to 15 dB or higher, significant low-frequency noise pollution is present according to acoustic standards.
