How loud is 85 dB?

85 dB is the pivotal boundary in acoustic health where sound stops being merely loud and becomes potentially hazardous to human hearing over prolonged exposure. Common comparisons include standing near heavy traffic from a sidewalk, running a food blender or garbage disposal, a noisy restaurant kitchen, or a busy workshop floor.

What 85 dB sounds like in daily life

At 85 dBA, normal conversation is drowned out. You must raise your voice or shout to be understood by someone standing three feet (one meter) away. Compared to normal office speech at 60 dB, 85 dB carries approximately 316 times the acoustic sound energy and sounds more than four times as loud to the human ear.

Typical sound sources that sit around 85 dB include:

  • Food blenders and food processors operating on standard kitchen counters.
  • Heavy diesel traffic and congested city roads heard from the curb or inside a vehicle with open windows.
  • Commercial lawn mowers heard from approximately 10 to 15 meters away.
  • Print shops, milling machines, and light manufacturing assembly lines without sound dampening.
  • Busy restaurant dining halls and bars during peak weekend dinner rush.

Note on frequency weighting: Occupational noise criteria use A-weighting (dBA), which filters out inaudible low-frequency rumble to match human ear sensitivity. Low-frequency thuds and subwoofers are better assessed with C-weighting (see dBA vs dBC distinction on the accuracy guide).

OSHA vs NIOSH Allowable Daily Exposure

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) both identify 85 dBA as the benchmark for occupational noise risk, but calculate allowable exposure times differently:

Sound Level (dBA) NIOSH REL (3 dB Rule) OSHA PEL (5 dB Rule)
85 dBA 8 hours (Recommended limit) 16 hours (Action Level trigger)
88 dBA 4 hours ~10.6 hours
90 dBA ~2.5 hours (151 min) 8 hours (Permissible limit)
91 dBA 2 hours ~7 hours
94 dBA 1 hour ~4.6 hours
97 dBA 30 minutes ~3 hours
100 dBA 15 minutes 2 hours

Note on OSHA Table G-16 values: Values marked with ~ (~10.6h, ~7h, ~4.6h, ~3h) are interpolated calculations based on OSHA's statutory 5 dB exchange rate formula (T = 8 / 2^((L-90)/5)), not directly printed rows in the original OSHA 1910.95 Table G-16. The official OSHA standard directly specifies 90 dBA at 8 hours and 100 dBA at 2 hours.

NIOSH uses the scientifically grounded 3 dB exchange rate (halving allowable exposure time for every 3 dB increase), whereas OSHA regulations enforce a 5 dB exchange rate. Both require employers to provide hearing protection once workplace noise averages 85 dBA TWA.

Frequently Asked Questions About 85 dB

How loud is 85 dB?

85 dB is loud enough that you must raise your voice to speak with someone one meter (three feet) away. Common everyday examples include standing near heavy city traffic, a kitchen blender running on high, a food processor, a commercial milling machine, or a lawn mower at a distance of about 10 meters.

Is 85 dB safe for hearing, and how long can you be exposed?

85 dBA is the internationally recognized threshold where occupational hearing conservation programs begin. According to NIOSH and CDC, the maximum safe unprotected exposure time at 85 dBA is 8 hours per day. With every 3 dB increase (the 3-dB exchange rate), safe exposure time is cut in half: 4 hours at 88 dB, 2 hours at 91 dB, and 1 hour at 94 dB.

What is the difference between OSHA and NIOSH limits at 85 dB?

OSHA (29 CFR 1910.95) sets 85 dBA as the Action Level that triggers mandatory hearing monitoring and baseline audiometric testing, while its permissible exposure limit (PEL) is 90 dBA over an 8-hour shift using a 5 dB exchange rate. NIOSH sets a stricter Recommended Exposure Limit (REL) of 85 dBA for 8 hours using a scientifically validated 3 dB exchange rate.
