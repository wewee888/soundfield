How loud is 120 dB?

120 dB represents the physiological threshold of pain for the human ear. At this acoustic intensity, sound ceases to feel like mere hearing and causes physical ear discomfort, tickling, or sharp pain. Familiar comparisons include emergency sirens at close range, heavy pneumatic rock drills, front-row concert speaker stacks, and jet engine operations.

What 120 dB sounds like

Compared to a conversational level of 60 dB, 120 dB carries 1,000,000 times more sound energy (a million-fold increase) and is perceived by human hearing as roughly 64 times as loud. Speech communication is completely impossible; shouts cannot be heard even inches away.

Common real-world sound sources operating around 120 dB include:

  • Emergency vehicle sirens (police, ambulance, fire truck) heard from 1 to 2 meters distance.
  • Pneumatic jackhammers, rock drills, and heavy riveters during unshielded construction work.
  • Front-row line-array speaker stacks at indoor rock concerts or stadium music festivals.
  • Jet aircraft takeoff heard from approximately 100 meters (or unshielded tarmac ground crew operations).
  • Stock car racing and drag racing pit lanes without acoustic barriers.

Acoustic note: At 120 dB, both A-weighting (dBA) and C-weighting (dBC) read similarly high for broadband sound, but deep low-frequency bass from concert subwoofers will register even higher on dBC (see dBA vs dBC comparison).

Hearing Damage & Exposure Limits

At 120 dBA, hearing damage does not require months of repeated exposure—it can occur almost immediately. Acoustic trauma can permanently damage the delicate stereocilia hair cells inside the inner ear cochlea, causing irreversible sensorineural hearing loss and chronic tinnitus (ringing in the ears).

Acoustic Danger Threshold:

Under the NIOSH recommended 3 dB exchange rate, the maximum allowable unprotected daily exposure at 120 dBA is under 9 seconds (approx. 7.5 seconds). At 115 dBA, allowable time is under 30 seconds.

In occupational settings, OSHA and MSHA regulations prohibit continuous worker exposure above 115 dBA without mandatory hearing protection devices. Anyone exposed to 120 dB environments must wear certified high-NRR earmuffs, fitted earplugs, or dual protection.

Frequently Asked Questions About 120 dB

How loud is 120 dB?

120 dB is exceptionally loud and corresponds to the beginning of the human threshold of pain. Common examples include standing within 1 to 2 meters of an emergency vehicle siren (police car or ambulance), a pneumatic rock drill or jackhammer up close, standing directly next to stadium concert speaker stacks, or a jet aircraft takeoff heard from 100 meters away.

Can 120 dB cause immediate hearing damage?

Yes. Sound levels at 120 dBA can cause immediate auditory fatigue, temporary threshold shift (TTS), or permanent acoustic trauma and irreversible tinnitus within seconds. Under NIOSH criteria (3 dB exchange rate), allowable unprotected daily exposure at 120 dBA is less than 9 seconds. Hearing protection (such as high-attenuation earmuffs or dual protection) is mandatory.

Can a smartphone microphone accurately measure 120 dB?

No. Most smartphone and laptop MEMS microphones suffer physical membrane saturation, acoustic clipping, and automatic gain compression above 110 to 115 dB SPL. At 120 dB, a phone reading will typically underestimate the true peak sound pressure level. Measuring sound levels this extreme requires a certified Class 1 or Class 2 sound level meter equipped with a high-SPL microphone capsule.
